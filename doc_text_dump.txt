[52] ABSTRACT
[53] Blood transfusion and organ transplantation are essential to modern healthcare, yet the management of donor information in many health facilities remains manual and fragmented. Paper-based records, disconnected systems, and reliance on personal networks to locate compatible donors lead to delays, wasted blood stock, and missed opportunities to save lives. This project set out to address these challenges by designing and developing the Centralised Blood and Organ Donor System (CBODS), a web-based platform that provides a single, secure registry of donors and enables hospitals to manage blood requests, inventory, and organ donation requests in one place. The system was built iteratively using the Agile methodology and implemented with the Django web framework, Python, a relational database, and HTML, CSS and JavaScript.
[54] CBODS supports four user roles the  administrator, hospital staff, donors and patients  and provides automated ABO and Rh compatibility matching, first-expiry-first-out blood bag reservation with expiry tracking, an emergency broadcast mechanism for urgent shortages, structured organ donation workflows, real-time notifications, and a complete audit trail. The system was evaluated through 110 automated tests covering unit, workflow, access-control, privacy and end-to-end scenarios, all of which passed. The results demonstrate that a centralised donor matching platform can be built with open-source technologies and that it offers a practical foundation for improving the speed and reliability of blood and organ donor coordination across health facilities.

[194] INTRODUCTION
[195] Background To the Study
[196] Blood transfusion is one of the most essential services in modern healthcare. Patients who lose blood through accidents, surgery, childbirth, or conditions such as severe anaemia and sickle cell disease often depend on donated blood to survive. Unlike medicines, which can be manufactured, blood and blood products cannot be produced artificially; they can only come from human donors (American Red Cross, n.d.). This makes the availability of willing and eligible donors a matter of life and death in every health system.
[198] The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that blood transfusion saves lives and improves health, but many patients who require transfusion do not have timely access to safe blood (World Health Organization, 2026). The same report shows a wide gap between countries in the rate of blood donation: in 2023 there were 28.9 donations per 1,000 people in high-income countries, compared with 8.5 per 1,000 in lower-middle-income countries and 4.5 per 1,000 in low-income countries (World Health Organization, 2026). Countries such as Ghana, which fall within the lower-middle-income group, therefore face a constant challenge of collecting and managing a blood supply that meets the needs of their population. WHO further recommends that blood collection, testing, processing, storage and distribution be coordinated at the national level through integrated blood supply networks (World Health Organization, 2026), which underlines the importance of well-organized donor management rather than scattered, facility-level efforts.
[200] Organ donation is equally important. For patients with end-stage failure of organs such as the kidney, liver or heart, transplantation may be the only treatment that can restore their health. Like blood donation, organ donation depends on voluntary donors and on careful matching between the donor and the recipient, and it involves highly sensitive personal and medical information that must be handled with care.
[204] In practice, donor information in many health facilities is still recorded on paper or kept in separate, unconnected systems belonging to individual hospitals or blood banks. A donor who registers at one facility is effectively invisible to another, so a hospital facing a shortage of a particular blood group has no reliable way of knowing where compatible donors or blood stock exist. Records can be duplicated, lost or damaged, blood bags can pass their expiry date without anyone noticing, and requests for blood in an emergency may depend on telephone calls and manual searching rather than on accurate, up-to-date information. Organ donation information is managed even less formally, with no structured way to record a donor’s consent, the organs they are willing to give, or the progress of a request.
[206] It was against this background that the project team set out to develop the Centralised Blood and Organ Donor System (CBODS). The aim of the system is to provide a single, centralised platform on which blood and organ donors can register, hospitals can manage their blood inventory and donation requests, and patients can be matched with available blood and compatible donors more quickly and reliably than is possible with manual records. The remainder of this chapter states the problem the project addresses, the objectives it set, and the scope of the work.
[207] Statement Of the Problem
[208] Blood and organ donation services in Ghana operate largely through individual health facilities, each of which keeps its own records of donors, donations and available blood. This fragmented arrangement creates several practical problems.
[209] First, there is no single registry of donors. When a patient needs blood of a rare or less common group, the hospital can only work with the donors it already knows or with whatever stock it happens to hold. There is no central view of which donors across different hospitals are registered, eligible and available, so suitable donors may never be contacted even when they are willing to help. Second, matching is done manually. Blood requests have to be checked by eye against recorded blood groups, and an error in that check, or a delay in performing it, can put a patient at risk. Third, blood inventory is difficult to track. Blood bags have a limited shelf life, and when
[211] expiry dates are recorded on paper, bags can expire unnoticed and be wasted, while the hospital continues to believe it has enough stock. Fourth, organ donation is handled without any formal structure: there is no standard way to record a donor’s consent, no clear request and approval workflow, and no audit trail of decisions. Finally, donor records are sensitive, and paper or duplicated records increase the risk of unauthorised access to personal and medical information.
[212] In response to these problems, the project designed and developed CBODS, a web-based system that keeps a centralised record of blood and organ donors, supports automated matching of blood requests against compatible available stock, and provides registered hospitals with structured tools for managing donations, inventory and requests, all within clearly defined roles and with an audit trail of important actions.
[213] Aim and Objectives
[214] Aim
[215] The aim of this project is to design and implement a centralised blood and organ donor system that provides a single, secure platform for donor registration, automated matching of blood requests, and hospital-level management of blood inventory and donation requests.
[216] Objectives
[217] Design and implement a centralised registry in which blood and organ donors can register once and have their details recorded, verified and managed from a single platform.
[218] Implement automated blood-group compatibility matching so that patient blood requests are checked against compatible available  blood stock and eligible  registered donors.
[219] Provide hospital staff with tools to record donations, mange blood-bag inventory, track expiry date, and reserve and issue blood for approved  request.
[220] Research Questions
[221] The project sought to answer the following questions:
[222] 1. What challenges do hospitals and donor coordinators face under the current manual and fragmented approach to donor registration and the matching of blood and organ donors to patient needs?
[223] 2. How can a centralised web-based system improve the speed and reliability with which patients are matched with compatible blood and organ donors?
[224] 3. How can such a system protect the privacy of donor records and provide accountability for actions taken within it?
[225] 1.5 Significance of Study
[226] The system is expected to benefit several groups. For donors, it offers a single place to register, a clear view of their own records, and the confidence that their personal information is handled securely. For patients, it promises faster access to compatible blood, particularly in urgent and emergency situations, because matching no longer depends on manual searching. For hospital staff, it removes the burden of paper registers, gives an accurate, real-time picture of available blood stock and its expiry dates, and records every important action for accountability. For the broader health sector, the project demonstrates a practical model of how donor information can be coordinated across facilities, which supports the direction WHO recommends of nationally integrated blood supply networks (World Health Organization, 2026).
[227] The project is also of academic value. It applies concepts from requirements analysis, database design, system architecture and software testing to a real problem, and the resulting system and documentation serve as a working example of how a centralised donor matching platform can be built with open-source web technologies.
[228] 1.6  Scope and Limitations
[229] The project covers the design, development and testing of CBODS, a web-based system with the following functional scope: donor registration and identity verification; administrator approval of donor registrations; donor screening records; recording of donations; management of blood-bag inventory including expiry tracking; patient blood requests classified as routine, urgent or emergency; automated blood-group compatibility matching; reservation and issuing of blood bags; organ donation requests with review and approval; notifications to users; and an audit log of system actions. The system serves four categories of users: the Administrator, Hospital Staff, Donors and Patients.
[230] Although the project's primary focus is on blood donation management, the system also includes an organ donation module. This module allows approved donors to register their willingness to donate specific organs and to indicate the hospital they wish to support. Hospital staff can review, approve or reject organ donation requests, and donors are notified of the outcome. However, the organ donation workflow is not the central contribution of this project; it is implemented as a supplementary feature to demonstrate that the platform can accommodate donation types beyond blood. The system does not perform organ compatibility matching, manage transplant logistics, or interface with transplant coordination services. A full organ donation management system would require integration with national transplant registries, tissue-typing laboratories and clinical workflows that are beyond the scope of this project.
[231] The project is limited in several ways. It is an academic project developed as a working prototype, so the data used to demonstrate and test it is demo data rather than real patient or donor records. The system is not connected to laboratory equipment, to existing hospital information systems, or to the systems of any national blood service, and it is not a nationwide deployment. It does not perform medical screening, make medical decisions, or manage the physical logistics of transporting blood or organs between facilities. Deployment in this project is limited to a local development environment, and a production deployment would require additional work on hosting, data protection and integration with official health systems. These limitations do not reduce the usefulness of the project as a demonstration of the proposed solution.

[235] LITERATURE REVIEW
[236] 2.1   Introduction
[237] This chapter reviews the literature that informed the design and development of the Centralised Blood and Organ Donor System (CBODS). It begins with an overview of blood donation, covering the blood group system and the donation and screening process, followed by an overview of organ donation. It then reviews existing donor management systems and related academic work, discusses the challenges of manual and fragmented donor records, and examines the technologies relevant to the proposed solution. The chapter closes with the conceptual framework adopted by the project. The review draws on official publications of the World Health Organization (WHO), the Ghana National Blood Service (NBS) and the Ghana Ministry of Health, together with peer-reviewed journal articles and current research on blood services in Ghana. Only sources that could be verified against their published texts are cited.
[238] 2.2 Theoretical Framework
[239] Blood transfusion and organ transplantation are among the most critical interventions in modern healthcare. Blood transfusion supports emergency care, surgery and childbirth, and is used in the management of chronic conditions such as severe anaemia and sickle cell disease (Obeagu and Alsadi, 2025). Because blood cannot be manufactured, every unit used in a transfusion must come from a human donor (American Red Cross, n.d. a). The WHO notes that blood transfusion saves lives and improves health, but that many patients who require transfusion do not have timely access to safe blood (World Health Organization, 2026). In 2023, whole-blood donation rates were 28.9 donations per 1,000 people in high-income countries, compared with 8.5 per 1,000 in lower-middle-income countries and 4.5 per 1,000 in low-income countries, revealing a persistent disparity in access to safe blood (World Health Organization, 2026).
[240] The theoretical foundation of donor management rests on several established principles. First, blood safety depends on a stable base of regular, voluntary, unpaid donors, which the WHO identifies as the safest and most reliable source of supply (World Health Organization, 2026). Second, every donated unit must be traceable to its donor, screened for transfusion-transmissible infections, and managed through careful inventory control, because donated blood has a short shelf life and must be replenished constantly (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.). Third, organ donation requires explicit consent, ethical oversight and protection of donors, as set out in the WHO Guiding Principles on human cell, tissue and organ transplantation (World Health Organization, n.d.). These principles — traceability, screening, inventory discipline and ethical consent — form the theoretical basis for the design of CBODS, which centralises donor records, automates compatibility matching, tracks blood-bag expiry dates, and structures the organ donation workflow with review and approval.
[241] 2.2.1 Blood Group and Compatibility
[242] Safe transfusion depends on matching the blood of the donor to the blood of the patient. The ABO blood group system, first described in the early twentieth century, classifies individuals into four main groups, A, B, O and AB, according to the antigens present on their red blood cells. People naturally produce antibodies against the A and B antigens they do not possess, so a patient given incompatible blood can suffer a serious reaction; for this reason ABO typing is carried out before every transfusion (Romanos-Sirakis & Desai, 2025). Within the ABO system, individuals with group O blood are considered universal donors of red cells, because their cells carry neither A nor B antigens, while individuals with group AB are universal recipients (Romanos-Sirakis & Desai, 2025). Combined with the Rhesus (Rh) factor, the ABO groups give rise to the eight common blood groups recorded in donor registries. These rules of compatibility are fixed and well documented, which makes them suitable to be encoded in software so that a blood request can be checked against available stock automatically rather than by eye.
[243] 2.3 Blood Group Systems and Compatibility
[244] Safe transfusion depends on matching the blood of the donor to the blood of the patient. The ABO blood group system, first described in the early twentieth century, classifies individuals into four main groups, A, B, O and AB, according to the antigens present on their red blood cells. People naturally produce antibodies against the A and B antigens they do not possess, so a patient given incompatible blood can suffer a serious reaction; for this reason ABO typing is carried out before every transfusion (Romanos-Sirakis and Desai, 2025). Within the ABO system, individuals with group O blood are considered universal donors of red cells, because their cells carry neither A nor B antigens, while individuals with group AB are universal recipients (Romanos-Sirakis and Desai, 2025). Combined with the Rhesus (Rh) factor, the ABO groups give rise to the eight common blood groups recorded in donor registries: O negative, O positive, A negative, A positive, B negative, B positive, AB negative and AB positive.
[245] These rules of compatibility are fixed and well documented, which makes them suitable to be encoded in software so that a blood request can be checked against available stock automatically rather than by eye. This approach is reflected in the software engineering literature: More et al. (2024) describe a blood compatibility matching algorithm that uses a decision tree over the ABO and Rh types, returning a positive match for compatible combinations and a denial for incompatible ones, and note that the rules are efficient and reliable but do not extend to rarer antigen profiles beyond ABO and Rh. The compatibility rules are also hierarchical: a patient with group AB positive can receive from any donor group, while a patient with group O negative can receive only from O negative donors. This hierarchy means that the rarer groups face greater scarcity, which in turn makes efficient matching and inventory management more important. CBODS encodes these rules as a decision table so that when a patient submits a blood request, the application immediately identifies which blood groups in the hospital's inventory are compatible, without requiring hospital staff to perform the check manually.
[246] 2.4 The Donation and Screening Process
[247] Blood donation is not open to everyone. The National Blood Service Ghana requires prospective donors to be between 17 and 60 years of age, to weigh at least 50 kilograms, and to be in good health, and it conducts a brief medical screening before donation, including a check of blood pressure and of the donor's haemoglobin level (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.). Donors found to be ineligible at screening are deferred, temporarily or permanently, and are given counselling and advice (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.). These requirements exist to protect both the donor and the recipient, and the WHO recommends that all donated blood be screened for transfusion-transmissible infections and that a stable base of regular, voluntary, unpaid donors be maintained as the safest source of supply (World Health Organization, 2026).
[248] The laboratory stage of the process is as important as the clinical stage. The National Blood Service Ghana screens every donated unit for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV and syphilis, in addition to ABO and Rhesus grouping, before the unit is labelled and released (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.). A unit that reacts to any of these tests is excluded from the supply, and the event is recorded. This laboratory gate testing before release is a safety requirement that any software managing blood inventory must represent, which is why CBODS stores a laboratory screening record for every donation and permits a blood bag to become available only when the screening outcome permits it.
[249] Screening also operates in two stages in practice. The initial eligibility assessment checks the donor's age, weight and the interval since the last donation, and a clinical screening records the donor's haemoglobin level and blood pressure. Research supports the automated application of such rules: Zulfikar et al. (2018) applied decision tree and Naive Bayes classifiers to classify prospective donors by age, sex, blood pressure and haemoglobin, motivated by the inefficiency of manual eligibility assessment at the Indonesian Red Cross, while More et al. (2024) describe a two-stage donor eligibility algorithm in which basic criteria such as age and weight are checked first and complex health metrics follow. The outcome of each screening must be recorded and linked to the donor's record, so that only donors who have passed both stages are eligible to donate. This two-stage process is a key design requirement of CBODS, which implements it as a service function applied at the point of screening and again at the point of donation recording.
[250] 2.5 Organ Donation
[251] Transplantation of human cells, tissues or organs is, in many cases, the best and sometimes the only way to save the lives of people with serious or life-threatening conditions (World Health Organization, n.d.). Organs for transplantation are obtained through voluntary donation, either from living donors during their lifetime or from deceased donors after death (World Health Organization, n.d.). The WHO observes that transplantation can restore essential functions and improve quality of life; kidney transplantation, for example, offers better outcomes than long-term dialysis for patients with kidney failure, and for patients with chronic liver failure or certain heart and lung conditions transplantation may be the only available treatment (World Health Organization, n.d.).
[252] Access to transplantation is highly unequal across countries, and the WHO lists among the obstacles to its development the lack of national strategies, inadequate legislation and ethical regulation, weak donation and transplantation infrastructure, and low public awareness. The shortage of organs is itself a serious concern, because it can give rise to unethical practices such as organ trafficking (World Health Organization, n.d.). These concerns are addressed internationally through the WHO Guiding Principles on human cell, tissue and organ transplantation, most recently updated in 2020, and through the 2024 resolution of the World Health Assembly calling on member states to strengthen regulatory frameworks, protect living donors from harm, and raise public awareness (World Health Organization, n.d.).
[253] Software support for organ donation remains comparatively simple in the systems described in the literature. Perumal and Magesh (2025) implemented a web application for organ donation in which donors register through a dedicated portal, submit personal and medical details, and direct their donation request to a chosen hospital, whose staff review the request through a separate secure login and update its status as pending, requested or approved; the donor then sees the real-time status of the request. This pattern donor submission, hospital review and status feedback is the workflow adopted by CBODS, which records the organ, the hospital and the outcome of each request, notifies the donor at each stage, and writes an audit entry for every decision.
[254] 2.6 Ghana's Blood Service and Existing Systems
[255] Blood services in Ghana are coordinated nationally. The National Blood Service Ghana is responsible for collecting, testing, storing and distributing blood, and it operates donation centres through which donors register and give blood (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.). A national information system, the Blood Safety Information System (BSIS), was launched in Ghana in 2017, making Ghana the second country in Africa, after Lesotho, to adopt it. BSIS is an open-source system designed to manage donor and blood safety information from the point of donor registration, through donation collection, laboratory testing, component processing and labelling, to storage and distribution to hospitals (Ministry of Health, Ghana, 2017). The launch of BSIS was accompanied by significant growth in the national service: annual blood collections by the NBS Ghana rose from about 140,000 units in 2010 to more than 160,000 units by 2016 (Ministry of Health, Ghana, 2017). BSIS demonstrates that Ghana has already begun to computerise blood services, but its focus is the national blood centre value chain rather than the day-to-day needs of individual hospitals and patients.
[256] An examination of the National Blood Service Ghana's public web service confirms both the progress and the remaining gaps. The website offers an online donor sign-up form whose stated purpose includes matching new registrations to existing donor records, a self-assessed eligibility quiz, a published weekly donation schedule for the Greater Accra region, an online adverse-reaction reporting form, and downloadable paper forms for facility registration, donor clinical records and blood transfusion requests (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.). However, the site offers no online appointment booking — attendance is by walk-in or by telephone — no donor accounts through which a donor could view their own history or next eligible date, no public or hospital-facing view of blood stock, and no working online blood request form for hospitals. These observations align closely with Head et al. (2026), who report that in 2024 the National Blood Service collected 187,280 units of blood, well short of the WHO-recommended annual requirement of about 308,000 units, that voluntary donations fell from 40 to 29 per cent of the total in 2024, leaving hospitals heavily dependent on replacement donors, and that when a hospital cannot reach suitable donors quickly it falls back on phone calls, personal networks and social media messages. They argue for a hospital-linked digital platform with location-aware matching and clear, traceable records (Head et al., 2026).
[257] Beyond BSIS, several other digital health initiatives in Ghana provide context for the type of system CBODS represents. The District Health Information Management System (DHIMS2) is a nationwide platform used by the Ghana Health Service to collect, aggregate and report health data from all public health facilities across the country (Ghana Health Service, 2023). DHIMS2 tracks disease surveillance, outpatient attendance, maternal health indicators and resource utilisation, but it does not manage donor information or blood inventory at the facility level. Its existence demonstrates that Ghana's health sector has the infrastructure for digital data collection and reporting, which means a blood donor management system could integrate with or complement DHIMS2 for reporting purposes.
[258] The Lightwave Health Information Management System (LHIMS) is an electronic health records system that has been implemented in several hospitals in the Greater Accra Region and other parts of Ghana (Lightwave Health, 2022). LHIMS allows healthcare providers to manage patient records, prescriptions, laboratory results and billing electronically. While LHIMS handles patient data comprehensively, it does not include modules for blood donor registration, blood bag inventory or compatibility matching. The adoption of LHIMS in multiple facilities shows that Ghanaian hospitals are increasingly prepared to use digital tools in their daily operations, which reduces the barrier to adopting a system such as CBODS alongside existing clinical record systems.
[259] The NBS Ghana has also partnered with technology organisations to develop mobile and web-based solutions for blood donation. BloodLink, a digital platform developed in collaboration with the NBS Ghana and technology partners, enables potential donors to register, locate nearby donation centres and receive reminders about when they are eligible to donate again (National Blood Service Ghana, 2023). BloodLink focuses on donor recruitment and engagement rather than on hospital-level inventory management or request fulfilment, but its existence confirms the direction of travel towards digital donor management in Ghana and demonstrates public acceptance of technology-mediated blood donation services.
[260] At the institutional level, the Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, which is the largest hospital in Ghana and the main referral centre in the Greater Accra Region, has implemented a blood bank management module within its hospital information system to track blood units collected, tested, issued and discarded (Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital, 2022). This module helps the blood bank at Korle-Bu manage its stock internally, but it operates within a single facility and does not share data with other hospitals or with the NBS Ghana's national system. A hospital that runs out of a particular blood group cannot query Korle-Bu's system to find out whether that group is available there, which illustrates the fragmentation that CBODS is designed to overcome.
[261] The University of Ghana Medical Centre (UGMC), which opened in 2018 as a modern tertiary hospital, has invested in an integrated hospital information system that includes laboratory information management and radiology modules (University of Ghana Medical Centre, 2023). While UGMC's system handles clinical workflows efficiently, blood donor management and cross-facility blood matching remain outside its scope. The experience of UGMC shows that even newly built, well-resourced hospitals in Ghana do not have a system for managing donor information or matching blood requests across facilities, reinforcing the case for a dedicated centralised platform such as CBODS.
[262] 2.7 International Systems: A Comparison of Maturity
[263] To situate CBODS within international practice, the public web services of four blood donation organisations were examined directly: the American Red Cross, the National Blood Service Ghana, the blood donation programme of the Philippine Department of Health and the Philippine Red Cross. The four form a maturity ladder of donor-facing digital services.
[264] The American Red Cross represents the most complete implementation. Its web service provides donor accounts, online appointment scheduling for blood drives, a digital pre-donation health history questionnaire (RapidPass) that may be completed only on the day of donation and in private, with the final eligibility determination reserved for the on-site screening (American Red Cross, n.d. b), a mobile application through which donors view their donation history and health results and track the journey of their donation to a hospital, a portal for organisations hosting blood drives, and a separate online ordering service for hospitals. Each donated unit carries a barcode that links the donor, the unit, the test samples and the hospital record, providing end-to-end traceability. Notably, even this mature system offers no interactive eligibility check before booking donors self-screen against static published rules and it addresses blood donation only, with no organ donation component.
[265] The National Blood Service Ghana, as described in Section 2.6, occupies an intermediate position: online pre-registration and self-assessment exist, but booking, donor accounts and stock visibility do not. The Philippine Red Cross presents the opposite extreme: it operates a large physical network of more than eighty blood facilities organised in a national-regional-chapter hierarchy, and it defines a clear paper workflow in which a patient presents a blood request form issued by a hospital or physician at any facility (Philippine Red Cross, n.d.), but its web presence provides no online registration, no booking and no stock information, and its eligibility criteria are scattered across news posts rather than consolidated. The Philippine Department of Health's National Voluntary Blood Services Program is primarily a policy and regulatory layer: its statute mandates the testing of every unit and establishes a "walking blood donor" registry of pre-qualified voluntary donors who can be called upon in communities without blood-banking facilities (Philippines, 1994), a legal precedent for exactly the standing, searchable donor registry that CBODS implements digitally, but the programme's public web presence offers no donor-facing functions at all.
[266] Three observations from this comparison shaped the design of CBODS. First, no examined service including the most mature lets a donor check eligibility interactively before attending, which is the gap CBODS's two-stage screening addresses at registration and at the point of donation. Second, hospital requests are handled by telephone or paper everywhere except within the American Red Cross's separate hospital portal, confirming that digitising the request workflow is a genuine contribution rather than a duplication. Third, none of the four services combines blood and organ donation in one system; the organ workflow in CBODS follows the donor-submission and hospital-review pattern identified in Section 2.5.
[267] 2.8 Review of Related Academic Systems
[268] Beyond the official services, academic prototypes provide directly comparable points of reference, because they address the same problem domain with similar constraints.
[269] Abdul-Gafaru et al. (2024) designed and implemented an online blood donation system that provides an accessible, efficient and secure platform through which donors and recipients can be linked. The system connects donors and recipients but does not manage hospital-level blood inventory or provide an organ donation workflow. Obeagu and Alsadi (2025), reviewing blood banking systems across Africa, identify digital platforms for donor recruitment as one of the promising innovations in the region, alongside mobile collection units and low-cost screening technologies, and note that national blood transfusion services provide a model of centralised management of blood stocks, ensuring more equitable distribution across a country. This supports the case for a centralised approach to donor information, which is the central idea of the present project.
[270] On the algorithmic side, More et al. (2024) studied the algorithms required by a blood bank management system and described three of them: a compatibility matching algorithm built as a decision tree over the ABO and Rh types; a request prioritisation algorithm that orders requests by urgency so that scarce stock is allocated to the most critical cases first; and a two-stage donor eligibility algorithm combining rule-based checks on age and weight with a machine-learning classifier for complex health metrics. Zulfikar et al. (2018) similarly classified donor eligibility using decision tree and Naive Bayes classifiers. These studies confirm that the decision logic CBODS implements the compatibility matrix, the urgency levels of routine, urgent and emergency, and the two-stage eligibility rules follows recognised approaches in the literature. CBODS deliberately uses transparent rule-based logic rather than machine-learning classification, because a prototype intended for hospital demonstration must be able to explain every decision it makes, and because the rule thresholds are configurable values that the operating institution controls.
[271] In the organ donation domain, Perumal and Magesh (2025) provide the closest comparable prototype. Their application, built with the Flask framework, implements donor registration, hospital selection, hospital-side review of donation requests through a secure staff login, and real-time status display to the donor. CBODS adopts the same interaction pattern but embeds it in a shared platform that also manages blood donation, screening, inventory and requests, with a role-based access partition between the two domains and an audit log of every decision — elements the reviewed prototype does not describe.
[272] 2.9 Challenges of Manual and Fragmented Donor Management
[273] Where donor information is kept on paper or in separate systems belonging to individual facilities, several problems recur. First, retrieval is slow: records cannot be searched instantly, so in an emergency hospital staff must rely on memory, telephone calls or asking patients' families to find donors, as Head et al. (2026) describe for Ghana. Second, records are vulnerable: paper registers can be lost or damaged, and duplicate entries can accumulate when the same donor registers at more than one facility, with no single source of truth. Third, because donated blood has a short shelf life and must be constantly replenished (National Blood Service Ghana, n.d.), a facility that cannot track expiry dates reliably will waste blood it believes to be usable.
[274] At the continental level, Obeagu and Alsadi (2025) identify inadequate infrastructure, limited funding, insufficient training of staff and low public awareness of voluntary donation as the main constraints on blood banking in Africa, and note that many countries rely heavily on family replacement donations, which are less reliable than voluntary, non-remunerated donations. The Ghanaian experience matches this pattern: the decline in voluntary donations reported by Head et al. (2026) means more patients depend on relatives and friends to supply blood at the moment of need, which is unpredictable and places the burden of searching on families at the worst possible time.
[275] Manual handling of donor and donation records also carries risks for safety and privacy. If a donation cannot be traced reliably to its donor, or if screening results are not linked to the correct records, the safety of the blood supply is weakened. Donor information, particularly identity documents and medical history, is sensitive, and records held on paper or duplicated across facilities are harder to protect from unauthorised access. WHO guidance on blood safety emphasises coordinated national systems with quality-assured screening and good documentation (World Health Organization, 2026), while the WHO principles for transplantation stress consent, ethical oversight and protection of donors (World Health Organization, n.d.). A system such as CBODS, which centralises records, automates matching, and keeps an audit trail of actions, responds directly to these concerns.
[276] 2.10 Review of Relevant Technologies
[277] The proposed system is a multi-user web application: donors, patients, hospital staff and the administrator all access the same platform through a web browser. Web applications are well suited to this situation because they require no software installation on each user's machine, they can be reached from any connected device, and a single deployed copy serves all users.
[278] The project uses the Django web framework, written in Python. Django is a full-featured framework that provides, out of the box, an object-relational mapping layer for database access, a template system for generating pages, a forms framework, an authentication system, and built-in protection against common web attacks such as cross-site request forgery and clickjacking (Django Software Foundation, 2026). Its testing tools allow automated tests to be written and run as part of development (Django Software Foundation, 2026). These facilities match the needs of CBODS closely: the data model can be defined once and shared with the database; compatibility rules and business logic can be enforced in application code; and the framework's security defaults help protect donor records. Data is stored in a relational database, which suits the structured records the system manages, such as donors, donations, blood bags and requests, and the relationships between them.
[279] The choice of technologies is discussed more fully in Chapter Three, where the architecture of the system is described. The purpose of this section is to record that the technologies employed are established, well documented and appropriate for a system of this type.
[280] 2.11 Summary
[281] This chapter has reviewed the theoretical foundations of blood and organ donation, the science of blood group compatibility, the donation and screening process, organ donation requirements, Ghana's blood service and its digital initiatives, the public services of four international blood organisations, related academic systems, the challenges of manual and fragmented records, and the technologies relevant to the proposed solution. It found that blood safety depends on voluntary donors, laboratory screening and careful inventory management; that organ donation demands consent, ethical review and sensitivity; and that Ghana has begun to computerise its national blood service through BSIS but still faces a wide gap between the blood collected and the blood needed. The comparison of systems — from the American Red Cross down to single-prototype academic implementations — showed that no existing system combines donor registration, compatibility matching, hospital inventory, laboratory screening records, emergency broadcast and organ workflows in one platform. The next chapter describes how CBODS was designed to fill these gaps, beginning with the methodology and the requirements of the system.

[296] 3.1   Methodology
[297] The project followed the Agile methodology, applied through an iterative and incremental development process, consistent with the approach outlined in Chapter One. The Agile approach delivers a system in small working increments, tests each increment as it is completed, and refines the design as understanding grows, rather than attempting to finish all requirements, design and implementation in one long sequential pass. Rather than building the whole system at once, the team divided the work into phases, each of which added a working part of the system, and each phase was tested before the next began. This methodology was chosen because the requirements of the system touch several distinct areas, such as donor management, inventory, blood requests and organ donation, which could each be built and tested as a separate increment, and because an iterative process allowed the team to keep a working version of the system available throughout development and to adjust the design in the light of what each phase revealed. The alternative, the waterfall model, was considered but not adopted, because the waterfall model requires every requirement to be fixed before design and implementation begin and testing to be carried out only at the end of the project. The project instead benefited from testing each phase as it was completed and from the flexibility to refine earlier parts of the system as later phases were built.
[298] The work proceeded through the following phases. In the first phase, the team analysed the problem and produced the requirements recorded in Section 3.2, together with the initial design of the data model and the architecture. The second phase implemented the foundation of the system: the user accounts and roles, the registration of donors, and the administrator’s approval workflow. The third phase added donor screening and the recording of donations, together with the blood bag inventory and expiry tracking. The fourth phase implemented blood requests, the automated compatibility matching, and the acceptance, rejection and fulfilment workflows, including the emergency broadcast. The fifth phase added the organ donation workflow, notifications and the audit log, and the final phase focused on the administrative dashboards, reporting, and an end-to-end test of the major workflows.
[299] Each phase ended with testing of the new functionality, and defects found during a phase were corrected before the next phase began. The testing approach is described in detail in Chapter Four, but two points are worth noting here because they shaped the design. First, the automated tests of the system are written against the same workflows that users follow, from registration through to the fulfilment of a request, so the tests act as a specification of the system as well as a check on it. Second, because the system handles operations such as reserving and issuing blood bags that must not go wrong even under concurrent use, the design and the tests gave particular attention to the atomicity of these operations, as discussed in Section 3.3.2. The technologies used throughout the development were the Django web framework with the Python programming language, a relational database, and HTML, CSS and JavaScript for the user interface; all of these are free, open-source tools, which also made the project economically feasible, as noted in the following section.

[353] 3.2.2   Stakeholder Analysis
[354] The stakeholders of the system are the individuals and organisations that have an interest in it or are affected by it. [384] 3.2.3 Functional Requirement
[385] The functional requirements state what the system must do. They are grouped here according to the module of the system to which they belong.
[386] Registration and verification: The system shall allow a user to create an account with a username, email address and password, and to choose a role, with donors and patients able to register themselves and hospital staff accounts created by an administrator. A registered donor shall be able to submit a donor profile containing their full name, date of birth, sex, blood group, weight, city, contact telephone number, medical history and a scanned copy of a government-issued identity document. The system shall validate the uploaded document, accepting only the permitted file types and a maximum size, so that invalid uploads are rejected at the point of entry. On submission, the donor’s registration shall enter a pending state for review by an administrator.
[387] Approval workflow: The system shall present pending registrations to an administrator, who shall either approve the registration, making the donor available in the system, or reject it with a stated reason. The donor shall receive a notification of the outcome, and a rejected donor shall be able to correct their details and resubmit the registration for a fresh review.
[388] Donor screening: Hospital staff shall be able to run a two-stage screening on an approved donor before any donation. The first stage applies the standing eligibility rules derived from the donor’s record, namely age, weight and the minimum interval since the donor’s last donation. The second stage records the donor’s haemoglobin level and blood pressure as entered by staff at the time of screening. The system shall store the outcome of each screening as eligible, temporarily deferred or ineligible, together with the reasons for any deferral, and shall notify the donor of the outcome.
[389] Donation recording: Hospital staff shall be able to record a completed donation for an eligible donor. The system shall enforce eligibility at the time of recording, so that a donation cannot be recorded for a donor whose registration is not approved, who has no recent eligible screening, or who has not yet waited the required interval. Each recorded donation shall create a corresponding blood bag in the hospital’s inventory with a computed expiry date.
[390] Inventory management: The system shall maintain, for each registered hospital, a record of every blood bag and its status. The possible statuses of a bag are available, reserved, issued, expired and discarded. The system shall calculate stock levels from the available bags rather than storing a separate counter, so that the displayed stock is always consistent with the underlying records. It shall identify bags that are close to their expiry date, mark bags that have passed their expiry date, and notify hospital staff when the available stock of any blood group falls below a defined threshold.
[391] Blood requests and matching: A patient shall be able to view the registered hospitals that are visible to them, see which blood groups each hospital currently has available, and submit a blood request stating the blood group, the number of units and the urgency of the request (routine, urgent or emergency). The system shall record the request as pending. Hospital staff shall be able to review the requests received by their hospital and either accept a request, which reserves the required number of compatible bags using the soonest-expiry-first order, or reject it with a reason. An accepted request shall be fulfilled by issuing the reserved bags, and the patient shall be notified at each stage. The system shall check blood requests against the rules of ABO and Rh compatibility, and shall provide hospital staff with a compatibility check that matches a proposed donor against a patient’s blood group and, when a donor is not compatible, suggests compatible alternatives. Where an emergency request cannot be met from available stock, the system shall send an urgent broadcast notification to the compatible donors registered in the hospital’s city.
[392] Organ donation workflow: An approved donor shall be able to submit an organ donation request indicating the hospital they wish to support and the organ they are willing to donate. The system shall record the request as pending for review by the staff of the chosen hospital, who may mark it as requested, approved or rejected. The donor shall be notified of the outcome of the review.
[393] Notifications: The system shall notify users of events concerning them, including registration outcomes, screening results, the status of blood requests and organ donation requests, low-stock warnings and emergency broadcasts. Notifications shall be stored in the system and shown in a notification list, and shall also be sent as email when the user has provided an address.
[394] Administration and audit: The administrator shall have an overview of the whole system, including the numbers of donors, available bags, pending requests and recorded donations, and a dashboard of charts showing how these figures are distributed. The system shall keep an audit log of important actions, recording who performed each action, what was changed and when, and the administrator shall be able to search the log and export reports on donors, donations, requests, blood bags and the audit log itself as CSV files.
[395] 3.2.4   Non – Functional Requirement
[396] In addition to what the system must do, the analysis identified how the system should behave. These non-functional requirements guided the design and the choice of technologies.
[397] Security: Access to every part of the system shall be controlled according to the user’s role, so that a user can only reach the functions permitted for that role. Identity documents uploaded by donors shall not be publicly accessible; they shall be readable only through an administrator-restricted view. The system shall protect against common web attacks, including cross-site request forgery, and shall log users out after a period of inactivity. Password requirements shall be enforced at registration, and password reset links shall expire quickly.
[398] Reliability: Operations that change the state of stock or requests, such as reserving bags for a request or issuing them at fulfilment, shall be performed as single atomic transactions, so that a partial failure cannot leave the stock or the request in an inconsistent state. The system shall prevent a bag from being reserved or issued twice, even when two users act at the same time.
[399] Performance: List pages, including the donor search, the request inbox and the audit log, shall be paginated so that the browser is not asked to render very long lists at once. Aggregate figures such as stock levels and system totals shall be computed by the database in single queries.
[400] Usability: Each role shall have a dashboard that shows the information most relevant to that role, and every action in the main workflows shall produce a clear message and, where appropriate, a notification, so that the user always knows what the system has done.
[401] Maintainability: Business rules such as the thresholds in Table 3.2 shall be configurable values rather than scattered constants, and the application shall be organised into separate modules, each responsible for one area of the system, so that changes are easy to make and to test.
[402] Portability: The system shall run in a local development environment using a file-based database, and the data layer shall be built with an object-relational mapping so that the system could be moved to a different database engine if a full deployment were undertaken.

[408] 3.3.1  Architectural Design
[409] CBODS is a three-tier web application, in which the user’s browser, the application server and the database are separate layers, as shown in Figure 3.2. The presentation tier consists of the web pages generated by the application, built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript, which run in the user’s browser and communicate with the server over HTTP. The application tier is the Django application itself: it receives requests, applies the business rules of the system, and prepares the pages shown to the user. The data tier is a relational database in which all records of donors, hospitals, blood bags, requests and the audit log are stored. This separation of concerns means that each tier can be developed, tested and replaced independently, and it matches the natural way in which a multi-user system of this kind is built.
[411] Figure 3.2: Three-tier system architecture
[412] Within the application tier, Django follows the model-template-view pattern, an adaptation of the well-known model-view-controller (MVC) architecture. The models define the structure of the data and its relationships; the views contain the logic that responds to a request; and the templates define how the data is presented to the user. In addition, the project rioritiz its business logic into a service layer: the rules of screening, the recording of donations, the reservation and issue of bags, and the writing of audit records all live in service modules that the views call. Keeping business rules out of the views means that the same rule is enforced wherever it applies, and that the rules can be tested independently of the web interface.
[413] The application is organised into eight modules, each responsible for one area of the system: Accounts (user accounts, roles, registration, login and password rules); Hospitals (the registered hospitals and the staff profiles that link staff accounts to a hospital); Donors (donor profiles, identity documents, registration status and screening records); Inventory (donations and blood bags, including stock levels, expiry and low-stock warnings); Requests_app (patient blood requests, the request lifecycle and the compatibility matching); Organs (organ donation requests and their review); Notifications (in-system notifications and emails); and Audit (the audit log, system totals and administrative reporting). Each module defines its own data model and its own set of pages, and the modules communicate through the services and the data model rather than through shared page logic.
[414] The automated matching described in the requirements is implemented as a dedicated component within the requests module. Given a recipient’s blood group, the component first identifies the set of acceptable donor groups using the compatibility rules of Table 3.1. It then applies that set in two ways: against the hospital’s blood bags, to find the available compatible bags in soonest-expiry-first order, and against the registered donors, to find approved, available donors with a compatible group. When a request is accepted, the required number of compatible bags is reserved from the available stock; the order in which bags are chosen follows the first-expiry-first-out (FEFO) principle, so that the bag closest to expiring is used first. For donor suggestions, the component ranks compatible donors by giving preference to donors in the same city as the hospital, and then to donors who are eligible to donate soonest. For emergency requests, donors who can donate immediately are ranked first, because the need is immediate.
[415] 3.3.2 Database Design
[416] The data of the system is stored in a relational database whose design follows directly from the requirements. Figure 3.3 shows the entity relationship diagram of the database.
[417] Figure 3.3: Entity relationship diagram of the database
[418] The database that supports CBODS is a relational database containing eleven tables, and its structure follows directly from the workflows described in the functional requirements. At the centre of the design is the User table, which stores the account of every person who interacts with the system their username, email address, password hash, assigned role (Administrator, Hospital Staff, Donor or Patient), phone number and active status. Each role-specific profile is held in its own table linked to the User table: a Donor table stores the donor's full name, date of birth, sex, blood group, weight, city, contact number, medical history, a reference to the uploaded identity document, the registration status (pending, approved or rejected), availability and any rejection reason; and a StaffProfile table links a staff account to the hospital they work for, ensuring that their access is scoped to that hospital alone. The Hospital table itself records each registered facility's name, city, address, phone number, list of services offered, organ donation requirements and a flag that controls whether the hospital is visible to non-administrator users.
[419] Donor management is supported by a ScreeningRecord table and a Donation table. The ScreeningRecord captures the outcome of each two-stage screening of a donor: whether the first stage of eligibility based on the donor's age, weight and donation interval was passed, the haemoglobin level and blood pressure readings recorded at the second stage, the overall outcome (eligible, temporarily deferred or ineligible), and the reasons for any deferral. The Donation table records each completed donation with its date, volume in millilitres, the donor who gave it, the hospital where it was recorded and the staff member who recorded it. The BloodBag table represents a single unit of donated blood and stores its blood group, volume, collection date, expiry date (computed as the collection date plus thirty-five days), current status (available, reserved, issued, expired or discarded), the donation from which it was created, and if the bag has been allocated the blood request for which it is reserved. The BloodRequest table holds a patient's request, capturing the requesting patient, the target hospital, the blood group needed, the number of units, the urgency level (routine, urgent or emergency), the current status (pending, accepted, rejected or fulfilled), any rejection reason and the timestamp. For organ donation, the OrganDonationRequest table records a donor's offer, specifying the donor, the hospital they wish to support, the organ type (kidney, liver, heart, lung, cornea, pancreas or skin), the review status (pending, requested, approved or rejected) and the timestamps of creation and decision.
[420] The remaining two tables handle communication and accountability. The Notification table stores in-system messages addressed to individual users, each with a subject line, body text, read status and timestamp, ensuring that every user receives timely updates about registration outcomes, screening results, request statuses and emergency broadcasts. The AuditLog table records every significant action taken in the system, capturing the identity of the actor (linked to the User table with a set-null constraint so that the entry survives even if the actor's account is later deleted), the action performed, the type and identity of the record affected, a JSON field for additional details, and the timestamp. The relationships between these tables follow from the workflows: a user may have at most one donor profile and at most one staff profile, both linked one-to-one; a hospital has many staff profiles, blood bags, donations, blood requests and organ donation requests; a donor has many screening records, donations and organ donation requests; each donation may yield blood bags; and each blood request may have bags reserved for it, linking the requests and inventory tables.
[421] Three design decisions protect the integrity of the data. First, nothing that can be computed from other records is stored as a separate counter: stock levels are counted from the available bags, a donor's age is derived from the date of birth, and the interval since a donor's last donation is derived from the donation records, so that no cached value can drift out of step with the records it summarises. Second, a blood bag that is reserved for a request is linked to that request by a foreign key, so that fulfilling one request can only ever issue the bags reserved for it and never another request's reservation. Third, the audit log records its subject by a loose reference to the type and identity of the record involved rather than a strict foreign key, so that an audit entry survives even if the record it describes is later deleted. Finally, the operations that change the state of the inventory and the requests  reserving bags for a request and issuing bags at fulfilment  both run as single database transactions with row-level locking, so that two users acting at the same time cannot reserve or issue the same bag twice. This design was chosen deliberately, because in a real hospital two staff members could act on the same request or the same stock at almost the same moment, and the system must behave correctly in that situation.

[423] The sequence diagram in Figure 3.4 illustrates the end-to-end lifecycle of a blood request in CBODS, from the moment a patient views the available hospitals through to the final fulfilment and notification. The diagram traces the interactions among five participants: the Patient, the CBODS Application, the Database, the Hospital Staff and the Notification Service. Each phase of the workflow is shown in a labelled group, and the sequence numbers correspond to the steps described in the text that follows.
[425] Figure 3.4: Sequence Diagram
[426] The workflow begins when a patient queries the system for registered hospitals and their available blood groups. The application retrieves this information from the database and displays it, after which the patient selects a hospital and submits a blood request specifying the blood group, number of units and urgency level. The application validates the request, creates a new BloodRequest record with a status of pending, and writes an entry to the audit log. A notification is then sent to the staff of the target hospital through the Notification Service, so that the request appears in their inbox without delay. When the hospital staff member reviews the request and decides to accept it, the application executes a single atomic transaction that locks the request row and the compatible blood bags using a database-level select-for-update operation. Within this transaction, the required number of compatible bags are located in first-expiry-first-out order, each bag is linked to the request and its status is changed from available to reserved, and the request status is updated to accepted. Because the entire operation runs inside one transaction, no bag can be reserved by two requests simultaneously, even if two staff members act at the same moment.
[427] If the hospital does not hold enough compatible bags to meet the request, the workflow enters the emergency branch. In this path the application searches the database for approved, available donors whose blood group is compatible with the request and who are registered in the same city as the hospital, and sends them an urgent broadcast notification through the Notification Service. This mechanism ensures that when stock is short the system can mobilise donors who are geographically close enough to respond quickly, rather than relying on manual telephone calls or personal networks. The final phase of the workflow is fulfilment: once bags have been reserved and are ready to be issued, the hospital staff member triggers the fulfil action, which runs a second atomic transaction that locks the reserved bags, verifies that each bag still carries a reserved status, changes the status of every bag to issued, updates the request to fulfilled, and writes the corresponding audit log entry. The patient receives a notification confirming that the request has been fulfilled, and the application checks whether the remaining stock of the issued blood group has fallen below the low-stock threshold, notifying the hospital staff if it has. This sequence ensures that every step from submission to fulfilment is recorded, that stock integrity is maintained under concurrent access, and that all parties patients, donors and staff are kept informed through notifications at each significant stage.

[443] 3.3.6 Security Consideration
[444] Because the system holds personal and medical information, including scanned identity documents, security and privacy were treated as central design concerns rather than as additions made at the end. The measures described in this section are implemented in the application and are verified by the tests described in Chapter Four.
[445] Role-based access control: Every page of the system that acts on data is restricted to the roles that are allowed to use it, and the restriction is enforced by the application on the server rather than by hiding links in the user interface. A patient cannot reach the pages of hospital staff, a donor cannot see the approval queue, and staff of one hospital cannot act on another hospital's records because the scope of a staff member's access is defined by the hospital to which their account is linked.
[446] Protection of identity document: A donor's identity document is never exposed as an ordinary media file. The document is stored outside the served web pages, and the only way to read it is through a view restricted to administrators, which streams the document to an administrator reviewing a registration. There is no public address from which the document can be downloaded, so the document cannot be reached by guessing a link.
[447] Privacy boundaries in queries: Beyond the role checks, the queries of the system are written so that each user only sees the data they are entitled to see. Patients see hospitals and their own requests but never donor identities; donors see their own records but never the details of other donors or of patients; and hospitals can be flagged as hidden, in which case they are invisible to everyone except administrators. Contact details of donors are shown only in the pages restricted to hospital staff and administrators.
[448] Session and password security: Sessions expire after fifteen minutes of inactivity, so that a logged-in session left unattended on a shared machine does not stay open. Passwords are subject to the standard checks against common, numeric and short passwords and against similarity to the username, and these rules are shown to the user at registration. Password reset links expire after one day. The application also enables Django's built-in protections against cross-site request forgery and similar web attacks by default.
[449] Audit trail: Every important action in the system is recorded in the audit log: who performed the action, what kind of record it concerned, and when it happened. This includes the approval and rejection of donors, the creation and status changes of blood bags, the acceptance, rejection and fulfilment of requests, and the progress of organ donation requests. The audit log is visible only to administrators, who can search it and export it, so that the history of the system can be reviewed and, if necessary, investigated.
[450] Deployment safeguards. Configuration values that matter for security, such as the secret key and the debug setting of the application, are read from the environment rather than fixed in code, so that a copy of the system installed elsewhere does not run with the developer's settings by accident.

[462] The Centralised Blood and Organ Donor System (CBODS) was developed using a stack of free, open-source technologies, chosen for their maturity, community support and suitability for a web-based application of this kind.
[463] Python was used as the primary programming language. Python’s clear syntax and extensive standard library made it well suited to implementing the business rules of the system, such as eligibility checks, compatibility matching and the reservation logic.
[464] Django web framework (version 5.2) was used to build the application. Django follows the model-template-view (MTV) architecture, an adaptation of the model-view-controller pattern, and provides built-in support for user authentication, form handling, session management, database migrations, and protection against cross-site request forgery (CSRF) and similar web attacks (Django Software Foundation, 2025). These features meant that the team could focus on the domain logic of donor management rather than reimplementing common web application infrastructure.
[465] SQLite was used as the relational database during development. SQLite is a file-based database that requires no separate server process, which made the system easy to set up and run on a single development machine. Because Django’s object-relational mapping (ORM) abstracts the database layer, the system could be moved to a more capable database engine such as PostgreSQL for production deployment without changing the application code.
[466] The front end of the system was built with HTML, CSS and JavaScript. The Bootstrap framework was used for layout, responsive grid design and pre-styled components, and the Inter typeface was used for consistent typography. Both Bootstrap and the Inter font are stored locally with the application rather than loaded from an external content delivery network, which means the system can run and be demonstrated without an internet connection. A custom stylesheet provides the light and dark colour themes, and a small JavaScript module manages the session-timeout countdown banner.

[489] 4.1.2 Implementation Details
[490] The system was implemented in six phases following the Agile methodology described in Chapter Three (Section 3.1). Each phase delivered a working increment of the system, and automated tests were written and executed for that increment before the next phase began. The phases and their outputs are described below.
[491] Phase 1:  Accounts, Donor Registration and Approval. The first phase established the user accounts and the four roles (Administrator, Hospital Staff, Donor, Patient), the registration page for donors, patients and hospital staff, and the administrator’s approval workflow for donor registrations. A user who chooses the Donor role is prompted to complete a donor profile containing their full name, date of birth, sex, blood group, weight, city, contact telephone number, medical history, and a scanned copy of a government-issued identity document. The system validates the uploaded document at the point of entry, accepting only image files (JPG, PNG) and PDF documents within a configured size limit; executable files and oversized uploads are rejected with a clear message. On submission, the donor’s registration enters a pending state. The administrator’s approval queue presents pending registrations in first-in-first-out order, with the ability to search by name. The administrator may view the donor’s identity document through a restricted view that streams the document to the browser without exposing it as a publicly accessible file. The administrator then approves the registration or rejects it with a stated reason. A rejected donor receives a notification and can correct their profile and resubmit without creating a new account. The hospital registration process follows the same pattern: a self-registration form captures the hospital’s details, the registration enters a pending state, and all system features are locked until the administrator approves the hospital.
[492] Phase 2:  Donor Screening and Donation Recording. The second phase implemented the two-stage screening process and the recording of donations. When hospital staff select an approved donor for screening, the system first runs the stage-one eligibility check, which evaluates the donor’s age, weight and the interval since the last donation against the thresholds configured in the application (see Table 3.2 in Chapter Three). The stage-one check returns one of three outcomes: eligible, temporarily deferred, or permanently ineligible. Hospital staff then enter the donor’s haemoglobin level and blood pressure readings for the stage-two clinical screening. The combined outcome of both stages is recorded as a screening record, and the donor is notified. Recording a donation enforces eligibility at the point of action: the system checks that the donor’s registration is approved, that a recent screening exists with an eligible outcome, and that the required interval since the last donation has elapsed. A successful recording creates a donation record and a corresponding blood bag in the hospital’s inventory, with the expiry date computed as the collection date plus the configured shelf life of thirty-five days.
[493] Phase 3:  Blood Bag Inventory and Expiry Tracking. The third phase added the inventory dashboard and the expiry tracking mechanism. The inventory is displayed on a per-hospital basis, with stock levels for each blood group computed from the available bags rather than stored as a separate counter. This design ensures that the displayed stock is always consistent with the underlying records. The system identifies bags that are within seven days of their expiry date and highlights them for attention. A daily management command scans all available bags and changes the status of any bag whose expiry date has passed to expired, recording the event in the audit log and sending a low-stock notification to the hospital’s staff when the available stock of any blood group falls below the configured threshold.
[494] Phase 4:  Blood Requests and Compatibility Matching. The fourth phase implemented the blood request workflow and the automated compatibility matching. A patient selects a hospital from the list of hospitals visible to them, and the system shows only the blood groups that the hospital currently has in available stock. The patient submits a request specifying the blood group, the number of units and the urgency (routine, urgent or emergency), and the request enters a pending state. Hospital staff see incoming requests in their inbox and may accept, reject or fulfil each request. Accepting a request reserves the required number of compatible blood bags from the hospital’s available stock, following the first-expiry-first-out (FEFO) principle: the bags nearest to their expiry date are reserved first. Each reserved bag is linked to the request that reserved it, which prevents one request’s fulfilment from issuing another request’s bags. Fulfilling a request changes the status of the reserved bags from reserved to issued and updates the request to fulfilled, and the patient receives a notification. When an emergency request cannot be met from available stock, the system sends an urgent broadcast notification to compatible donors registered in the hospital’s city, and presents hospital staff with a ranked list of compatible donors, rioritizing those who can donate immediately.
[495] Phase 5: Organ Donation, Notifications and Audit Log. The fifth phase added the organ donation workflow, the notification system and the audit log. An approved donor may submit an organ donation request indicating the hospital they wish to support and the organ they are willing to donate. Hospital staff review the request and may approve or reject it, and the donor is notified of the outcome. The notification system stores in-app notifications for every significant event and also sends emails when the user has provided an address. The audit log records every important action in the system, including who performed the action, what kind of record was affected, and when the action occurred.
[496] Phase 6:  Dashboards, Reporting and End-to-End Verification. The final phase focused on the administrative dashboards, the reporting and export features, and the end-to-end test of the major workflows. The administrator’s dashboard shows headline figures and distribution charts. Hospital staff can view a scoped summary of their hospital’s stock, donations and requests. Reports on donors, donations, blood requests, blood bags and the audit log can be exported as CSV files.
[497] 4.1.3 Testing and Quality Assurance
[498] Testing was carried out at two levels: automated tests written against the application’s service layer and views, and a manual review of the user interface against the requirements. The automated test suite is the primary quality assurance mechanism, because it exercises every branch of the business logic and every page of the interface, and it can be re-run in seconds whenever a change is made.
[499] The tests were written using Django’s built-in test framework, which runs each test in a separate database transaction that is rolled back at the end, so that tests do not interfere with one another and can be run in any order. The test suite falls into four categories.
[500] Boundary tests verify that the system applies its thresholds correctly at the exact edges. The donor screening tests confirm that a donor aged seventeen is rejected while a donor aged eighteen passes; that a donor aged sixty-one is marked as permanently ineligible; that a donor weighing 49.9 kilograms fails the weight check while one weighing 50.0 kilograms passes; and that a donor whose last donation was eighty-nine days ago fails the interval check while one whose last donation was ninety days ago passes. The clinical screening tests confirm that a haemoglobin reading of 12.4 g/dL is rejected while 12.5 g/dL is accepted, and that blood pressure readings at the boundaries of the acceptable range (systolic 90 to 180 mmHg, diastolic 60 to 100 mmHg) are accepted while readings one unit outside those boundaries are rejected.
[501] Workflow tests exercise the full lifecycle of a feature from the user’s first action to the final state. The compatibility tests verify the full ABO and Rh compatibility matrix: for each of the eight recipient blood groups, the system correctly identifies the set of compatible donor groups. The request lifecycle tests confirm that accepting a request reserves the correct number of bags in FEFO order; that fulfilling a request changes only the bags reserved for that request; that attempting to fulfil a request that has already been fulfilled raises an error; and that rejecting a request records the stated reason. The donation-blocking tests confirm that a donation cannot be recorded for a donor who has no screening, whose screening was deferred, or whose registration has not been approved. The end-to-end test exercises the complete demo story through the HTTP interface, following every step the way a real user would: the donor registers and uploads a government ID; the administrator reviews the ID and approves the registration; hospital staff run a screening that passes both stages; staff record a donation, which creates an available blood bag; a patient registers and requests a group the hospital has in stock; staff accept the request, which reserves the bag using FEFO; staff fulfil the request, which issues the bag and notifies the patient; an emergency request triggers the donor broadcast and presents compatible donor suggestions; a donor submits an organ donation request; hospital staff approve the request; and the administrator’s dashboard and audit log reflect every step.
[502] Access-control and privacy tests verify the role-based restrictions described in Chapter Three (Section 3.3.4). A donor is refused access to every hospital-staff URL. A patient is refused access to donor search, donor approval and organ review. Hospital staff of one hospital cannot see or act on another hospital's blood requests, organ requests or blood bags. A hospital that has been flagged as hidden is invisible to patients but visible to the administrator. The identity-document tests confirm that the document is readable only through the administrator's gated view; every other role receives a 403 forbidden response, and an unauthenticated request is redirected to the login page. The password-reset tests confirm that a known email address receives a reset link, that an unknown address reveals nothing, that the link can be used once and then expires, and that the new password must satisfy the configured validators. The session-timeout tests confirm that authenticated pages render a session deadline to the browser, that the cookie resets with each request, and that a session past its deadline is treated as logged out.
[503] Render tests load every page of the system for every role that is permitted to see it, including public pages (login, register, hospital registration, password reset), donor pages, patient pages, hospital staff pages, the hospital account's own pages, pending-hospital pages and administrator pages.
[504] The tests fail on any template error, including missing template tags, typos in filter names and unrendered template syntax that would appear as visible text in the browser.

All validation is repeated on the server, so a submission that bypasses the checks in the browser is still rejected.
[759] Figure 4.3;Donor profile
[760] DONOR PROFILE
[761] The donor profile form records the details on which the rest of the system depends: the donor's full name, date of birth, sex, blood group, weight, city, contact number, medical history and a scanned government-issued identity document. A donor completes the form once after registering, and the submission remains pending until an administrator reviews the identity document and approves or rejects the registration. A rejected donor uses the same form to correct the details and resubmit, which returns the record to the review queue, while an approved donor can reopen the form to keep details such as the contact number up to date. The identity document itself is never exposed as a public file; it can be viewed only by an administrator through a gated page.
[763] Figure 4.4;Patient notification
[764] PAITENT NOTIFICATION
[765] The notification centre stores every message the system generates for a user, including registration outcomes, screening results, blood request status changes, organ request decisions, low-stock warnings and emergency broadcasts. Notifications are listed newest first, and an unread counter beside the Notifications link in the sidebar makes new items visible from any page. A single control on the page marks all unread notifications as read at once. Where the user has supplied an email address, the same message is delivered by email in addition to appearing in the application.

[768] ADMIN DASHBOARD
[769] The administrator's charted dashboard gives an oversight of the whole platform: donors grouped by registration status, available bags by blood group and by hospital, and requests by status and by urgency. The page also lists the hospital registrations awaiting a decision, the most recent donations recorded anywhere in the system, and the newest hospitals to join. All of the figures are computed by the database in single queries, so the charts always reflect the current records. From the dashboard the administrator can move directly into the hospital review queue.
[771] Figure 4.6;System dashboard(admin role)
[772] SYSTEM DASHBOARD (ADMIN ROLE)
[773] The system dashboard is the page every user reaches after signing in, and for the administrator it shows the headline totals of the system: donors, hospitals, available bags, requests and donations, together with the counts of pending donor and hospital registrations and the most recent entries of the audit log. It answers what the state of the system is, while the charted dashboard shows how that state is distributed. Both pages read the same shared counting function, so their figures can never disagree. For the other roles, the same address serves the role-specific dashboards described next.

It shows whether the donor passes the first-stage eligibility rules at the present time, the outcome of the most recent screening, and, for a donor who has given blood before, the number of days remaining until the next donation is permitted. The page also lists the donor's recent donations and organ donation requests together with their hospitals and outcomes. The donor can therefore tell at a glance whether they are able to donate today.

It shows the hospital's available stock by blood group, highlights the groups that have fallen below the low-stock threshold, counts the bags approaching their expiry date, and lists the pending blood requests that await a decision. The hospital's own organisation account sees the same working view once it has been approved, whereas a hospital still awaiting approval sees only its dashboard and profile until the administrator approves it. This makes the dashboard the natural starting point for screening, inventory and request work.

The monthly series are reconstructed from the audit log entries of the underlying records, so a report always reflects the same events the audit trail holds. Staff can use the page to brief hospital management on collection and usage trends without preparing the figures by hand.
[786] 4.2.3   Performance Evaluation
[787] The system was evaluated for performance characteristics that matter in a multi-user, concurrent environment, even though it is deployed as a prototype rather than in production.
[788] Concurrency safety: The operations that change the state of inventory and requests — reserving bags for a request and issuing them at fulfilment — are performed as single database transactions with row-level locking. This means that two staff members acting on the same request or the same stock at the same time cannot reserve or issue the same bag twice. This was verified by the `RequestLifecycleTests.test_double_issue_race` test, which simulates a racing worker by attempting to fulfil a request that has already been fulfilled, and confirms that the system raises an error rather than issuing bags twice.
[789] Pagination: List pages, including the donor search, the request inbox and the audit log, are paginated so that the browser is not asked to render very long lists at once. The `PaginationTests` confirm that the donor search returns the correct number of records per page (25 per page) and that filter parameters survive pagination, so that a user who searches by city and then moves to page two retains their filter.
[790] Consistency: Stock levels are computed from the available bags rather than stored as a separate counter, so the displayed stock is always consistent with the underlying records. The age of a donor is derived from the date of birth, and the interval since the last donation is derived from the donation records, so no cached value can drift out of step with the data it summarises.
[791] Session management: Sessions expire after fifteen minutes of inactivity, reducing the risk of an unattended session being exploited on a shared machine. The `SessionTimeoutTests` confirm that the session deadline is rendered on authenticated pages, that the cookie resets with activity, and that expired sessions are redirected to the login page.
[792] Template integrity: The render tests confirm that every page renders without template errors for every permitted role. This is a form of quality assurance that directly affects the user experience, because a template error would present the user with an error page instead of the expected content.
[794] The system was not subjected to formal load testing or stress testing, because it is an academic prototype intended for demonstration rather than production deployment. A production deployment would require additional performance evaluation under realistic user loads.
[795] 4.3   Summary
[796] This chapter has described the implementation and evaluation of CBODS. The system was built in six phases using Python, the Django web framework, SQLite, and HTML, CSS and JavaScript, all of which are free and open-source tools. The implementation covered all eight modules of the system: accounts, hospitals, donors, inventory, requests, organs, notifications and audit. Testing was carried out through 110 automated test methods across 27 test classes, covering boundary conditions, full workflow lifecycles, access control, privacy of identity documents, password-reset security, session management, page rendering and an end-to-end walkthrough of the complete demo story. All tests passed. The evaluation against the three objectives stated in Chapter One confirmed that each objective was met, with supporting test evidence for every criterion, and the key features of the finished interface were presented as the user meets them, from the login page through the role dashboards to the hospital reports. The system demonstrates that a centralised donor matching platform is feasible with open-source web technologies and provides a foundation for coordinating donor information across health facilities. The next chapter summarises the project, draws conclusions from the work, and makes recommendations for further development.
[797] CHAPTER FIVE
[798] CONCLUSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS
[799] 5.1   Discussion
[800] 5.1.1 Comparison with Existing Systems
[801] The Centralised Blood and Organ Donor System (CBODS) was designed to address the gap identified in the literature review: the absence of a single platform that combines a centralised donor registry, automated compatibility-based matching, hospital-level blood inventory with expiry tracking, and a structured organ donation workflow.
[802] The Blood Safety Information System (BSIS), launched in Ghana in 2017, demonstrated that computerizing blood services is feasible and has contributed to growth in annual blood collections (Ministry of Health, Ghana, 2017). However, BSIS focuses on the national blood center value chain  from donor registration through testing, processing, labelling, storage and distribution  rather than on the day-to-day operations of individual hospitals and patients. CBODS complements this national infrastructure by addressing the facility-level needs that BSIS does not cover: a hospital's ability to search for compatible donors across locations, to manage its own blood stock and expiry dates, to receive and serve blood requests from patients, and to handle organ donation requests through a structured workflow.
[803] The online blood donation system designed by Abdul-Gafaru et al. (2024) provides a platform for connecting donors and recipients, and the continental review by Obeagu and Alsadi (2025) identifies digital donor-recruitment platforms as a promising innovation for Africa. However, neither of these systems addresses hospital-level inventory management or the organ donation workflow. CBODS differs from these systems in two further respects: it enforces automated ABO and Rh compatibility matching against both available stock and registered donors, and it includes an emergency broadcast mechanism that notifies compatible donors in a hospital's geographic area when stock is insufficient to meet an urgent request.
[804] A key distinction is that CBODS is not designed to replace BSIS or any existing national blood service. Rather, it is intended to demonstrate how a centralised, hospital-linked donor management platform can work alongside national infrastructure to improve the speed and reliability of donor matching at the facility level. The system does not manage laboratory testing, component processing or the physical logistics of blood distribution, which are responsibilities of the national service. This scope was chosen deliberately because the project is a prototype, not a production deployment, and its value lies in demonstrating a feasible architecture and set of capabilities that could be extended in future work.
[805] 5.1.2   Challenges Faced
[806] Several challenges arose during the development and evaluation of CBODS.
[807] Requirements complexity: The system spans eight modules, each with its own set of rules, workflows and user interactions. Translating the requirements of blood compatibility, donor screening, inventory management and organ donation into consistent application logic required careful coordination across the modules. For example, the eligibility rules defined in the configuration must be applied consistently in screening, donation recording, and the availability check that determines which blood groups a patient can request. Ensuring this consistency was an ongoing task throughout development, and the automated tests served as a safeguard against regressions as new modules were added.
[808] Concurrency and data integrity: Operations that change the state of blood bags  reserving bags for a request and issuing them at fulfilment  must be atomic and safe under concurrent access. In a system intended for multiple hospital staff to use simultaneously, two users acting on the same request or the same stock at nearly the same time must not cause data corruption, such as a bag being reserved for two requests or being issued twice. Designing the transaction logic to use row-level locking and verifying it with a dedicated concurrency test added complexity to the implementation, but it was necessary for the correctness of the system.
[809] Demo data and evaluation limitations: As an academic prototype, the system is populated with demo data rather than real patient, donor or hospital records. The evaluation is based on automated tests that simulate user actions through the HTTP interface, not on user acceptance testing with real hospitals, blood banks, donors or patients. This means the system has been shown to work correctly under controlled conditions, but its usability, performance under real-world loads and suitability for clinical environments have not been evaluated. A production deployment would require additional testing, performance evaluation and integration work.
[810] Integration with external systems: The system does not connect to laboratory equipment, existing hospital information systems or the national blood service infrastructure. In a real deployment, a centralised donor matching system would need to exchange data with these external systems to be practically useful, which involves standardised interfaces, data formats and authentication mechanisms that were beyond the scope of this project.
[811] 5.1.3   Lessons Learned
[812] The development of CBODS produced several practical lessons that are relevant to software projects of a similar kind.
[813] The Agile methodology suited the project well. By dividing the work into six phases and testing each phase before proceeding, the team was able to keep a working version of the system throughout development and to detect defects early. The alternative a sequential, waterfall approach  would have deferred testing to the end of the project, which would have made it much harder to identify and correct defects across the eight modules and the many interactions between them. The phased approach also made it possible to adjust the design of earlier modules as the requirements of later modules became clearer, without needing to restart the project.
[814] Automated tests paid for themselves quickly. Writing tests alongside the implementation, rather than as an afterthought, meant that each new feature was checked immediately and that defects in existing features were caught as soon as they were introduced. The end-to-end test, which walks through the complete demo story from registration to audit log, proved especially valuable as a check that the eight modules work together as intended.
[815] Separating business logic from presentation was essential. By keeping the rules of eligibility, compatibility, inventory and screening in a service layer separate from the views, the same rule was enforced wherever it applied, and the rules could be tested independently of the web interface. This separation also made it straightforward to test concurrent operations using Django's transaction and locking facilities, which would have been difficult to verify through the user interface alone.
[816] Privacy and access control must be designed in from the start. The role-based access control, the restricted handling of identity documents, and the privacy-aware queries that ensure each user sees only the data they are entitled to see were all designed and tested early in the project. If these controls had been added later, the risk of inadvertently exposing sensitive data through an overlooked page or query would have been significant.
[817] 5.1.4   Contributions to the Field
[818] The project makes several contributions to the practice of donor management and to the academic demonstration of system design.
[819] Firstly, it demonstrates a complete, working architecture for a centralised donor matching platform that integrates donor registration, automated compatibility matching, hospital inventory management, emergency broadcast and organ donation in a single system. This architecture provides a reference for developers and institutions that wish to build or adopt a similar platform.
[820] Again, it implements the first-expiry-first-out (FEFO) principle for blood bag reservation, ensuring that older stock is used before newer stock and reducing waste due to expired bags. This principle, combined with automatic expiry tracking and low-stock alerts, addresses a practical problem that manual systems handle poorly.
[821] Also, the system demonstrates how role-based access control, restricted handling of sensitive identity documents, and a complete audit trail can be implemented together in a Django application, providing a model for protecting donor privacy in a multi-user healthcare system.
[822] Lastly, the automated test suite of 110 tests across 27 test classes serves as a practical example of how to test a multi-module web application systematically, covering boundary conditions, workflow lifecycles, access control and privacy, and an end-to-end walkthrough of the major user journeys.
[823] 5.1.5   Future Work
[824] The following extensions would make CBODS more useful in practice and are recommended for future work.
[825] Integration with real hospital data and the National Blood Service. The most important next step is to connect CBODS with real donor and hospital data and, where possible, with the Blood Safety Information System (BSIS) maintained by the National Blood Service Ghana. This would require standardised data formats, secure application programming interfaces (APIs), and formal data-sharing agreements with the institutions involved. It would also require a thorough evaluation of data protection requirements under Ghanaian law.
[826] Production deployment and hosting. The current system runs in a local development environment using SQLite. A production deployment would require a more capable database such as PostgreSQL, a production-grade web server and hosting infrastructure. This would also require an evaluation of the system's performance under realistic user loads, including load testing and stress testing.
[827] Integration with laboratory systems. In a real hospital, blood screening results come from laboratory equipment, not from manual data entry. Integrating CBODS with laboratory information management systems would allow screening results to be imported automatically, reducing data entry errors and making the screening process faster.
[828] Mobile application. A mobile application would make the system more accessible to donors and patients, particularly in settings where mobile phones are the primary means of accessing the internet. A mobile application could also support push notifications for emergency broadcasts, which would be faster than in-app notifications.
[829] Advanced analytics and reporting. The current system provides basic dashboards and CSV export. Future work could add more advanced analytics, such as trend analysis of blood stock levels over time, prediction of future shortages based on historical data, and reporting on donor demographics to support targeted donor recruitment campaigns.
[830] Expanded organ donation workflow. The current organ donation workflow covers registration, review and approval. A more complete workflow would include the matching of organ donors to recipients, the recording of transplant outcomes, and the long-term follow-up of transplant recipients, in accordance with the WHO Guiding Principles on human cell, tissue and organ transplantation (World Health Organization, n.d.).
[831] 5.2   Conclusion
[832] This project set out to design and develop a centralised blood and organ donor system that provides a single, secure platform for donor registration, automated matching of blood requests, and hospital-level management of blood inventory and donation requests.
[833] The project began by analysing the challenges of manual and fragmented donor management, particularly in the Ghanaian context, where the gap between the blood collected and the blood needed remains wide and where hospitals often depend on phone calls and personal networks to find compatible donors. A review of existing systems showed that while national blood services have begun to computerise their operations, no single platform combines a centralised donor registry, compatibility-based matching, hospital inventory with expiry tracking, and a structured organ donation workflow.
[834] The system was designed with four categories of users in mind  the administrator, hospital staff, donors and patients  and was built in six phases using the Agile methodology. The implementation uses Python, the Django web framework, a relational database, and HTML, CSS and JavaScript, all of which are free and open-source tools. The resulting system provides role-based access control, automated compatibility matching using the ABO and Rh blood group systems, first-expiry-first-out blood bag reservation, emergency donor broadcasts, structured organ donation requests, notifications, and a complete audit trail.
[835] Testing was carried out through 110 automated tests covering boundary conditions, workflow lifecycles, access control, privacy of identity documents, password-reset security, session management, page rendering and an end-to-end walkthrough of the major user journeys. All tests passed, confirming that the system operates as intended.
[836] The project shows that a centralised donor matching platform is feasible with open-source web technologies and offers a foundation for coordinating donor information across health facilities. Future work should focus on integrating the system with real hospital data and the National Blood Service, deploying it in a production environment, connecting it with laboratory information systems, and developing a mobile application to improve accessibility. The project contributes a working reference architecture, a practical implementation of the first-expiry-first-out reservation principle, a model for donor privacy and auditability in a multi-user healthcare system, and a comprehensive test suite that demonstrates systematic testing of a multi-module web application.
